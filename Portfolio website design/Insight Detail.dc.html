The hidden cost of skipping UX research

Muhammad Babar· UI/UX Designer

"We don't have time for research" is the most expensive sentence in product development. The time is always spent — the only question is whether it's spent before launch, in a week of interviews, or after launch, in months of rework.

On one recent project, the team had already built a settings redesign twice. Both versions tested fine internally and failed with customers. A single week of interviews revealed why: users weren't looking for settings at all — they expected those controls inline, at the moment of use.

Where the cost actually lands

Skipped research doesn't show up as a line item. It shows up as support tickets that ask "where do I find…", as features with single-digit adoption, and as engineering sprints spent rebuilding flows that never matched how people work.

Research that fits a shipping schedule

Research doesn't need to be a quarter-long program. Five user interviews surface most major usability issues. A one-week discovery sprint — interviews, an audit of analytics, and a journey map — is usually enough to de-risk a quarter of design decisions.

The goal isn't certainty; it's cheaper mistakes. A prototype that fails in front of five users costs a week. The same failure in production costs a roadmap.

How to start this week

Pick the riskiest assumption on your roadmap and put it in front of five users. In practice that looks like:

Day 1–2Recruit five users and write a short discussion guide around one decision.
Day 3–4Run the sessions. Record them. Invite an engineer to watch at least one.
Day 5Synthesize into three findings and one decision. Ship the decision.

Common questions

How many users do I need for a usability test?

Five users surface most major usability issues. Beyond that, returns diminish quickly for a single design.

How long should a discovery sprint take?

One week: two days recruiting and planning, two days of sessions, one day of synthesis and decisions.
